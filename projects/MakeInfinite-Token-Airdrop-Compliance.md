# Overview

### Situation:
- MakeInfinite Labs preparing blockchain token launch requiring airdrop
- Tokens classified as currency; legal compliance critical
- I built data pipeline to aggregate data, apply rules, track audit

### Tactics:
- Founder sent me a list of wallets to add, outside established rules
- Initially prepared to accommodate additions, but thinking again, didn't know if I could
- Escalated founder’s request to legal counsel for validation
- Legal counsel confirmed this would be illegal

### Actions:
- Texted concern back to founder, who called and demanded I add them
- Escalated to email including founder and legal counsel
- Summarized situation, proposed compliant alternatives
    - Documented written trail of instructions and responses
- Legal council reinforced we "could not add" outside of defined rules
    - but we could add if they qualify for existing ruleset
- Founder later apologized for outburst, though objected to legal inclusion

### Results:
- Preserved audit trail integrity
- Protected self, company, and community from regulatory risk
- Reinforced ethical decision-making despite leadership pressure
- Had a very smooth airdrop experience otherwise

# Full Text

At MakeInfinite Labs I took on the extra responsibility of building out the process to “Airdrop” tokens for a new blockchain launch the company was doing.  Because tokens are the “currency” that drives the crypto economy, you need to seed your community with tokens to begin trading, which involves setting rules on how to earn, and then giving away the tokens on the day of launch. 

However, tokens also count as “currency” from the perspective of the US government, so there are many legal requirements about auditing and making sure rules can be validated.  i.e., you’re allowed to make up any arbitrary rule you want, but you absolutely must be consistent. 

I had taken up the role of authoring the data pipeline that accumulated data, applied rules, and aggregated amounts for the token Airdrop, and was rigorous about keeping a clean audit-trail and working with our chief legal council, to make sure I followed all rules and kept the company in compliance. 
Into this environment, one of the core founders sent me a note, telling me to add several users to the token list.   At first I just took the wallet addresses and created a new category for “other additions,” however, I also sent a note informing our legal council, to make sure this was acceptable.  Somewhat to my surprise, she said absolutely not – this would be blatantly illegal. 

Calling back to the other founder, I let him know his people would need to find a way to qualify via an existing channel.  He got very upset, demanded I simply add the people to the list, and disconnected.
I am far from a legal expert in crypto-law, but I know enough to listen to lawyers before technologists.  I crafted an email to both our chief legal council and the other founder, summarized the situation, made a few suggestions on where we might direct these extra folks to apply to the existing rules, so we could qualify them.  Our legal council replied similar to her earlier position, with a hard “no” but agreed with my assessment; I could add them if they followed existing rules.  The other founder did not reply. 

I obviously took the discussion to email for two reasons; (a) to facilitate the direct discussion, without being the go-between, and (b) to document in writing the direction how I was being instructed to proceed.   I was not willing to do something illegal, and I wanted to make that crystal clear to all participants – just in the nicest possible way. 

The founder called me the next day, and apologized for his previous outburst, however oddly expressed regret only that I included legal, rather than “working it out with us two.”   I re-emphasized that I wasn’t going to risk legal trouble, for me, my family, and the company, just to give several strangers a token drop.
